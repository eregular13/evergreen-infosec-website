OpenAI's OpenWorks: what small businesses should know, and five security questions to ask first

Free, hands-on AI help is coming to Main Street. It's a real opportunity. Here's how it works and what to check before you connect AI to your business.

On November 23, 2026, OpenAI launched OpenWorks, a program that pays local AI practitioners to work alongside small business owners at no cost to the business. The pilot is live in 20 U.S. communities, including Olympia and Seattle here in Washington.

We think this is good news for small businesses. We also think the conversation so far is missing something: almost nobody is talking about security. This article covers both.

How OpenWorks works

  • You pick the project. You choose something you want to get done, and an OpenWorks Partner works on it with you, one-on-one.
  • It's free for the business. OpenAI pays the partner a monthly stipend. Participating businesses also get three months of ChatGPT Business at no cost.
  • No technical experience needed. The aim is for you and your team to be able to use the tools on your own afterward.
  • Who qualifies. OpenAI says the pilot is open to independently owned U.S. small businesses, such as restaurants, retailers, service firms and manufacturers. Press coverage reports that venture-backed startups, private-equity-owned companies and software firms are excluded.

The examples OpenAI has shared are practical and small in scale: an inventory dashboard for a gift shop, faster vendor price checks for a sign shop, and better outreach to restaurants for a local grower. That's the right way to start with AI: one real task, done well.

What it doesn't cover

OpenWorks is built to get you started. It isn't a security review, and it has an end date. Three things are worth planning for:

  • Security and privacy. A coach's job is to make the tool useful. Checking what the tool can reach, and who else can reach it, is a different job.
  • What happens after the first project. The free software lasts three months. After that, someone has to pay for it, maintain what was built, and train new staff.
  • One vendor's tools. The program is built around OpenAI's products. They may well be the right fit, but it's worth knowing you have options.

Five security questions to ask first

Ask these whether you're working with an OpenWorks Partner, a consultant, or setting things up yourself.

1. What data will the AI be able to see?

Useful AI projects usually involve real business data: inventory, customer lists, emails, invoices. Before you connect anything, write down what the tool needs and what it doesn't. Customer payment details, employee records and anything covered by a contract or regulation deserve a deliberate yes or no.

2. Whose account is it, and who has access?

Set AI tools up under a business account you own, not a staff member's personal one, and turn on multi-factor authentication. If someone outside the business helps you set it up, know exactly what access they have, and remove it when the work is done.

3. Is your data used to train AI models?

This depends on the product and plan. Business plans and personal plans often have different defaults. Check the data settings for the plan you're actually on, and remember that staff using personal accounts for work may be on different terms entirely.

4. What can it do on its own?

There's a big difference between AI that drafts an email for you to review and AI that sends it. If a tool can send messages, change prices, place orders or edit records, decide where a person needs to approve the action first. Start with "AI drafts, people decide" and loosen it only where you're comfortable.

5. What happens in month four?

Before you build your business around a tool, know what it costs after the free period, who will fix it when something changes, and whether you can get your data out. Ask for a short written note on how the solution works, so you aren't dependent on one person's memory.

A simple rule of thumb: treat an AI tool like a new employee. Give it only the access it needs for the job, check its work, and write down what it's allowed to do.

A ten-minute checklist

  • AI tools are on a business-owned account with multi-factor authentication.
  • You have a short list of what data the tool may and may not be given.
  • You've checked the data and training settings for your plan.
  • Anything that sends, buys, deletes or changes records needs a person's approval.
  • Staff know which AI tools are approved for work, and which aren't.
  • Outside helpers' access is removed when their work ends.
  • You know the cost and the owner of the tool after the free period.

Our view

If you're eligible for OpenWorks, apply. Free, hands-on help from someone local is a good way to find out what AI can do for you. Just bring these questions with you.
